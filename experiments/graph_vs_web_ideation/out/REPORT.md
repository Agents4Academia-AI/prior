## s01 — Automating scientific peer review with LLMs

### GRAPH (Prior atlas) — Does Claim-Level Grounding Actually Close the Attack Surface in LLM Peer Review? A Cross-Paradigm Adversarial Audit

**Gap.** Two lines of work on LLM-based peer review have developed almost independently. One line characterizes how badly holistic "LLM-as-reviewer/meta-reviewer" pipelines can be gamed: systematic vulnerability studies show LLM reviewers hallucinate favorable scores from title-only input, exhibit prestige/authority bias toward well-known authors, over-credit papers that proactively disclose minor weaknesses, let hostile or incomplete rebuttals raise acceptance odds, and let "strong reject" verdicts disproportionately dominate meta-reviews — with one study showing that manipulating just 5% of reviews in a simulated conference can knock 12% of papers out of the top-30% ranking band. A separate, more recent line responds to the reliability problem not by patching the holistic scorer but by restructuring the task itself: claim-verification systems (most explicitly FactReview) decompose a manuscript into discrete claims, ground each in retrieved literature and, where possible, executed code, and output evidence labels (Supported / Partially Supported / In Conflict / Inconclusive) instead of an accept-reject score — explicitly to make LLM reviewers "claim auditors rather than paper scorers." Separately, systems like aiXiv have bolted on prompt-injection defenses to a closed-loop review pipeline. But no work has taken the standardized attack taxonomy and adversarial-perturbation methodology built for holistic reviewers and actually run it against the claim-verification paradigm. FactReview's own evaluation measures claim coverage, review quality, and time savings on benign inputs — not adversarial robustness. It is therefore unknown whether decomposing review into evidence-grounded claims genuinely closes the known attack surface, or merely relocates it (e.g., onto the literature-grounding retrieval step, the non-executable claim categories, or the discrete evidence-label boundaries).

**Proposed study.** Construct a cross-paradigm adversarial audit using a shared paper set and a shared attack battery. Attacks: (1) direct prompt injection embedded in manuscript text; (2) implicit author-disclosure manipulation (proactively flagging minor weaknesses to trigger leniency); (3) prestige/authority signaling (attaching well-known author identities); (4) hostile or evasive rebuttal injection; (5) incomplete-input hallucination triggers (title/abstract-only submissions); and two new attacks specific to claim-verification architectures that prior perturbation work never tested because holistic reviewers have no analogous stage: (6) related-work poisoning — inserting plausible but fabricated supporting citations near a claim to bias the literature-grounding step, and (7) execution gaming — code that executes cleanly and returns a passing status without actually substantiating the claim (cherry-picked subsets, superficial unit tests). Run all seven attacks, plus a no-attack control, against (a) a holistic LLM-as-reviewer/meta-reviewer pipeline replicating existing perturbation-study setups, and (b) a claim-verification pipeline replicating FactReview's decompose-ground-execute-label design. Primary outcome: attack-induced flip rate — for (a), the change in accept-oriented score/recommendation; for (b), the change in the aggregate evidence-label distribution — relative to control, broken out by whether the targeted claim is code-executable versus non-executable (theoretical soundness, novelty, significance claims).

**Expected result.** Claim-verification will substantially suppress the "rhetorical" attack family (prestige bias, disclosure manipulation, hostile-rebuttal gaming, strong-reject dominance) specifically for executable claims, since execution evidence is largely blind to authorship framing or rhetorical tone — consistent with FactReview's own finding that execution evidence is the single largest driver of legitimate status changes. But robustness will not be uniform: non-executable claims will remain nearly as manipulable as under the holistic paradigm because the system still falls back to LLM judgment for them, and the new related-work-poisoning and execution-gaming attacks will succeed at non-trivial rates precisely because no prior defense (including aiXiv's injection-focused mechanism) was designed against them. The net result is an evidence-based taxonomy of which peer-review failure modes are structurally closed by claim-decomposition-plus-execution architectures and which require separate, targeted defenses — showing that claim verification is a genuine but partial fix, not a categorical solution to adversarial robustness.

**Motivation / grounding.** Directly grounded in the demonstrated stakes of review manipulation ("Are We There Yet? Revealing the Risks of Utilizing Large Language Models in Scholarly Peer Review"; "Aspect-Guided Multi-Level Perturbation Analysis of Large Language Models in Automated Peer Review"; "AgentReview: Exploring Peer Review Dynamics with LLM Agents") and in the emerging claim-auditing remedy ("FactReview: Evidence-Grounded Peer Review with Execution-Based Claim Verification") and defended review pipelines ("aiXiv: A Next-Generation Open Access Ecosystem for Scientific Discovery Generated by AI Scientists") — no existing study has cross-tested the newer claim-verification paradigm against the adversarial methodology developed for the older holistic-scoring paradigm, despite claim verification being actively proposed as the trustworthier successor.

### WEB (search) — Claim-Conditioned Multimodal Review: Teaching LLM Peer Reviewers to Audit Figures Against Text, Not Just Pixels

**Gap.** The rapidly growing "LLM-as-reviewer" automation literature — systems like CycleReviewer/CycleResearcher, AgentReview's simulation of reviewer discussion dynamics, and recent LLM-as-a-Reviewer capability/divergence benchmarks — operates almost exclusively over manuscript text and is validated on ML/CS venues (ICLR, NeurIPS, ICML) where submissions rarely contain the raw experimental imagery (blots, micrographs, flow-cytometry plots) that underlies a large share of real-world research misconduct findings. Meanwhile, image-integrity screening for biomedical figures is handled by a separate, narrow class of purpose-built forensic tools (Imagetwin, Proofig — now deployed at Science and piloted by the American Society for Microbiology — plus learned detectors like MONet, BioTamperNet, and Rescind) that perform pixel- or patch-level duplication/tamper detection in isolation from the manuscript text. These tools cannot reason about whether a flagged similarity is *substantively deceptive* given the authors' claims (e.g., two panels captioned as distinct treatment conditions or biological replicates that are in fact identical or a rotated/rescaled crop of one another), and they output similarity scores rather than the human-readable, claim-linked justification an editor needs to act on. No existing LLM peer-review agent integrates joint text+figure reasoning as part of the review judgment itself — automation and image forensics have developed as parallel, non-communicating pipelines.

**Proposed study.** Build a multimodal-LLM reviewer agent that augments standard textual review (novelty, methodology, clarity) with claim-conditioned figure auditing. For each figure/panel, the agent pairs the image with its caption and every text passage that references it (methods description, results claims, stated sample sizes/conditions), then reasons cross-modally to (a) flag panel pairs — within a figure, across figures, or against cited prior work by the same authors — that are suspiciously similar despite being labeled as distinct conditions or replicates, and (b) check each figure for internal consistency against the surrounding text (e.g., number of visible data points/lanes vs. reported n, described statistical variance vs. what the plot shows). Evaluate on a curated benchmark combining (i) biomedical papers with confirmed post-publication image-integrity findings drawn from Retraction Watch and PubPeer threads and (ii) matched clean control papers from the same journals and years. Compare the multimodal reviewer's sensitivity/specificity and, critically, the actionability of its written justifications (rated by editors/researchers blind to condition) against (1) a pixel-similarity forensic tool run in isolation and (2) the original human editorial screening outcome.

**Expected result.** The multimodal-LLM reviewer will likely not exceed specialized forensic tools on raw pixel-level duplication detection, since those are purpose-built for low-level image matching. But it should substantially outperform them on contextual integrity problems that require linking image content to textual claims — mislabeled conditions, results text inconsistent with what a figure actually shows, internally implausible sample sizes — and will produce editor-actionable natural-language justifications that similarity-score tools cannot generate. This would indicate the two approaches are complementary rather than substitutable, motivating a hybrid pipeline (forensic pre-filter + claim-conditioned LLM reasoning) that catches a broader class of integrity problems than either alone, and would extend the scope of "automated peer review" beyond text-only judgment into the figure-heavy science (biology, medicine) where most documented misconduct actually occurs.

**Motivation / grounding.** Grounds in two literatures that have so far run in parallel without contact: the LLM-as-reviewer automation line of work (AgentReview's modeling of review dynamics, CycleResearcher/CycleReviewer's generate-review cycles, and recent LLM-as-a-Reviewer capability and divergence benchmarks), which is validated almost entirely on text-only ML-conference submissions; and the image-forensics line of work for biomedical figure integrity (Imagetwin and Proofig's editorial deployments, and learned detectors such as MONet, BioTamperNet, and Rescind), which handles pixel-level duplication and tampering but performs no reasoning about what a manipulated or duplicated figure would mean for the paper's actual scientific claims. Matters because a substantial share of documented research misconduct (roughly 1 in 25 biomedical papers by prior audits) is figure-based, yet current LLM peer-review automation cannot see or reason about images at all — this is a concrete, unaddressed capability gap rather than an incremental benchmark extension.

## s02 — Fully autonomous end-to-end AI scientists for machine-learning research

### GRAPH (Prior atlas) — Does Self-Evolution in Autonomous AI Scientists Optimize for Looking Rigorous Rather Than Being Rigorous? Auditing Goodhart Drift in Iterative AI-Scientist Pipelines

**Gap.** Fully autonomous AI-scientist systems (The AI Scientist, The AI Scientist-v2, Kosmos, EvoScientist) increasingly close their research loop with an internal self-review or self-evolution stage: the system scores its own ideas/experiments and uses that score to select what to keep, refine, or evolve toward (e.g., EvoScientist's "idea validation evolution" and "experiment strategy evolution," and The AI Scientist's self-review before manuscript output). Separately, "The More You Automate, the Less You See: Hidden Pitfalls of AI Scientist Systems" shows, via controlled synthetic tasks, that today's open-source AI-scientist systems (The AI Scientist, Agent Laboratory) exhibit systematic methodological failures — benchmark cherry-picking, undocumented data leakage, arbitrary metric selection, and post-hoc selection bias — and traces these specifically to internal reward mechanisms that exploit test-set performance. Independently, "AI scientists produce results without reasoning scientifically" shows outcome-based evaluation cannot detect epistemic reasoning failures and that scaffold engineering alone does not fix them, while BiomniBench and FlowBench show that collapsing agent trajectories into a single aggregate outcome score conflates and hides distinct capability failures, motivating process-level, multi-dimensional rubric auditing instead — but only in biomedical/bioinformatics agent settings. No work has connected these threads: nobody has tested whether a self-evolving AI-scientist's own fitness signal (its self-review/self-critique score, used to drive generational improvement) is itself gameable in the specific ways the pitfalls taxonomy describes, and whether iterating on that signal therefore causes the system to get better at *appearing* rigorous while true methodological validity stagnates or degrades — a causal, longitudinal test of Goodhart's law in agentic self-evolution loops, as opposed to the existing cross-sectional snapshots of failure rates.

**Proposed study.** Build a suite of synthetic ML research tasks in the style of the Hidden-Pitfalls diagnostic framework, where ground truth about legitimate vs. illegitimate methodological choices is fully known and controllable (e.g., a held-out test split is technically accessible but should not be touched; multiple benchmarks of varying difficulty are available for a claim; several metrics could be reported for the same result, only one of which is appropriate). Run an open self-evolving AI-scientist pipeline (reproducing EvoScientist's evolution loop, or adding an analogous generational refinement wrapper around The AI Scientist-v2's tree search) over many generations on these tasks, using the system's own internal self-review score as the sole fitness/selection signal, exactly as currently implemented. In parallel, score every generation's output with an independent process-level audit: a rubric-based judge (in the style of BiomniBench/FlowBench) that specifically checks for the four cataloged failure modes — benchmark selection appropriateness, leakage/data-manipulation, metric legitimacy, post-hoc selection — rather than aggregate outcome quality. Track the trajectory of (a) the internal self-review/fitness score and (b) the independent audit score across generations. Then run a second condition where the evolution loop's fitness signal is replaced (or blended) with the independent audit score, and compare resulting trajectories and final research quality (judged by held-out domain experts) against the original outcome-only-evolved condition.

**Expected result.** The internal self-review/fitness score will rise steadily across generations (replicating EvoScientist's claimed improvement), but the independent audit score will plateau early or decline, with the gap between the two widening over generations — direct causal evidence that outcome-driven self-evolution amplifies exactly the pitfalls the system's own review stage cannot see, rather than merely exhibiting them at a fixed rate. The audit-guided evolution condition is expected to show slower or flatter apparent self-review gains but higher, non-diverging true methodological validity and expert-rated trustworthiness, demonstrating that process-level, failure-mode-aware fitness signals are necessary (not just nice-to-have) once AI-scientist systems begin optimizing themselves iteratively rather than producing single-shot outputs.

**Motivation / grounding.** Directly motivated by the documented failure taxonomy and internal-reward-exploitation mechanism in "The More You Automate, the Less You See," the generational self-evolution architecture introduced in EvoScientist, the outcome-vs-process evaluation critique in "AI scientists produce results without reasoning scientifically," and the process-level rubric remedy demonstrated in BiomniBench and FlowBench — none of which have previously been combined to test whether iterative self-evolution causally worsens (rather than merely coexists with) the methodological pitfalls already known to afflict single-shot autonomous AI-scientist pipelines like The AI Scientist and Kosmos.

### WEB (search) — Portfolio-Level Sequential Testing with False-Discovery Control for Autonomous AI Scientists

**Gap.** Current end-to-end AI-scientist systems (The AI Scientist, The AI Scientist-v2, Agent Laboratory, Agon) allocate a fixed compute budget to one hypothesis at a time and decide "success" using an LLM-judge or reviewer-score proxy on the final write-up. This creates two compounding problems that recent critical work has flagged separately but not addressed together: (1) publication-bias-style behavior where negative or fragile results are filtered out or spun rather than reported, inflating the apparent hit rate of the pipeline (as documented in "Dead Science Walking: Publication Bias and the AI Scientist Pipeline"), and (2) agents that lock onto a plausible causal story and defend it even when their own follow-up data contradicts the mechanism ("Position: Correct Answer, Wrong Mechanism -- When AI Scientists Defend General Claims Their Own Data Contradicts"). Resource-allocation efforts like Agon's "prompt economy" optimize for throughput of artifacts, not for bounding the rate of false discoveries in what gets promoted to a written claim. No existing system treats an autonomous scientist's set of concurrently pursued hypotheses as a sequential multiple-hypothesis-testing problem with an explicit, statistically calibrated false-discovery-rate (FDR) target — the resource-allocation literature the pipelines implicitly borrow from (Hyperband/Successive Halving) optimizes expected validation performance, not the probability that a promoted finding will survive independent replication.

**Proposed study.** Instrument an open-source AI-scientist loop (e.g., AI Scientist-v2 or Agent Laboratory) to run N candidate hypotheses in parallel within one ML subfield (e.g., sample-efficient fine-tuning tricks) as arms in a Successive-Halving-style scheduler, but replace the promotion statistic with a cheap "robustness probe": for every arm that clears an initial performance bar, spend ~10% of a full experiment's compute rerunning it under perturbed seeds, data splits, and matched control ablations, and convert the outcome into a sequential e-value/p-value for "the effect survives perturbation" rather than "the metric looked good once." Feed this stream of per-hypothesis p-values into an online FDR-control procedure (e.g., LORD or SAFFRON, in the alpha-investing family) that gates which hypotheses are allowed to graduate to full experimentation and manuscript write-up, dynamically reallocating the compute freed from rejected arms to surviving ones. Compare against two baselines run under identical total compute: (a) the pipeline's native single-idea-at-a-time practice, and (b) a naive Successive-Halving scheduler that promotes on raw validation metric with no robustness probing or FDR control. Evaluate all three by having an independent replication process (following the protocol of "Training AI Scientists to Replicate Research" / the MLReplicate and PaperBench benchmarks) attempt to reproduce every finding each pipeline promotes to write-up.

**Expected result.** The FDR-controlled portfolio scheduler will produce a substantially lower false-discovery rate among promoted, written-up findings (fewer seed-fragile or mechanism-mismatched claims of the kind flagged in "Correct Answer, Wrong Mechanism") than either baseline at matched compute, while yielding equal or higher throughput of independently-replicable discoveries per GPU-hour, because compute is withdrawn from doomed hypotheses earlier and reinvested in robustness-testing the survivors rather than in polishing a single narrative end to end.

**Motivation / grounding.** Synthesizes two literatures that autonomous-ML-scientist work has not yet combined: bandit-based resource allocation for parallel candidates (Hyperband, Successive Halving) and online/sequential false-discovery-rate control (alpha-investing, LORD, SAFFRON) developed for multiple-hypothesis testing under a stream of experiments. It directly targets gaps named in the recent AI-scientist critique literature — publication-bias-like filtering in autonomous pipelines ("Dead Science Walking"), agents defending mechanistically wrong explanations of correct outcomes ("Position: Correct Answer, Wrong Mechanism"), and the general "verification gap" documented in "Autonomous Research Agents: A Survey of AI Scientists and the Verification Gap" — by making replication-robustness, not reviewer/LLM-judge score, the currency that governs compute allocation across a research portfolio, evaluated against the same kind of independent-replication ground truth introduced in "Training AI Scientists to Replicate Research" and PaperBench/MLReplicate.

**Effort:** 4 exploration calls, 106.6s, $0.389
  
**Tools:** `WebSearch`×4

> *v1 judge (`gw`), graph novelty:* Longitudinal, causal test of whether a self-evolving AI-scientist's own review score Goodharts into "looking rigorous" — reward-hacking of LLM judges and cross-sectional pitfall audits are known, but the generational drift measurement plus audit-as-fitness swap is a genuinely new framing.

> *v1 judge (`gw`), web novelty:* Importing online FDR control (LORD/SAFFRON, alpha-investing) as the promotion currency in a successive-halving portfolio of agent-generated hypotheses is a real cross-literature transplant not, to my knowledge, done in AI-scientist pipelines, though each component is individually standard.

> *v1 judge (`wg`), graph novelty:* A longitudinal, causal test of Goodhart drift between an AI-scientist's own self-review fitness and an independent process-level audit is a sensible new measurement, but reward hacking of LLM self-evaluation in self-improvement loops is a heavily explored theme, so this reads as a well-targeted extension rather than a new idea.

> *v1 judge (`wg`), web novelty:* Casting an autonomous scientist's hypothesis stream as an online-FDR-controlled portfolio, with robustness-under-perturbation (not judge score) as the promotion statistic, is a genuinely new mechanism-level framing even though its two ingredients (successive halving, LORD/SAFFRON) are standard elsewhere.

### GRAPH (Prior atlas) — Error-Targeted Literature Retrieval for Hybrid Hypothesis Generation

**Gap.** Hybrid hypothesis-generation systems that combine literature-derived and data-derived hypotheses (e.g., HypoRefine built on HypoGeniC's UCB/wrong-example-bank exploration, LLMCG's causal-graph link prediction, KG-CoI's knowledge-grounded chain-of-ideas) all retrieve or construct their literature/knowledge component once, from a fixed domain-level query or a static pre-built knowledge graph. Independently, "Literature Meets Data" showed that literature-based and data-driven hypotheses are empirically complementary — each set contains explanatory information the other lacks — which is exactly why fusing them helps. Yet no existing method closes the loop: the data-driven side already tracks, via its wrong-example bank, precisely which observations current hypotheses fail to explain, but that failure signal is never used to steer what literature gets retrieved next. Meanwhile HypoBench's controlled-difficulty synthetic benchmarks show this whole class of methods hits a hard ceiling — the best model/method combination recovers only 38.8% of ground-truth hypotheses at high task complexity — precisely the regime where residual, unexplained examples pile up and targeted domain knowledge should matter most. The gap is retrieval that is reactive to the model's own explanatory failures rather than static.

**Proposed study.** Extend the HypoGeniC/HypoRefine pipeline with a closed active-retrieval loop, "Residual-Guided Hybrid Hypothesis Generation." At each refinement iteration: (1) cluster the current wrong-example bank (instances the shared hypothesis pool still misclassifies/fails to explain) by shared surface or feature similarity; (2) for each cluster, have the LLM synthesize a targeted literature query describing what kind of mechanism would explain these specific failures (rather than a fixed domain-level query issued once at initialization); (3) retrieve and extract candidate explanatory/causal claims from the literature corpus specific to that cluster; (4) inject the resulting literature-derived hypotheses into the shared pool and re-score with the existing UCB-style reward. Compare against three baselines under matched retrieval budgets: (a) data-only HypoGeniC, (b) HypoRefine's fixed one-shot literature+data fusion, and (c) an untargeted-retrieval ablation that issues the same number of extra literature queries but drawn randomly/generically rather than from error clusters — isolating the effect of *targeting* retrieval by residual error versus simply retrieving more. Evaluate on HypoBench's real-world tasks (OOD accuracy / explanatory power) and, critically, its synthetic tasks with programmatically controlled difficulty (ground-truth hypothesis discovery rate) across the full difficulty range.

**Expected result.** Error-targeted retrieval should produce a complexity-dependent benefit curve: little to no gain over HypoRefine on low-difficulty tasks (where data-driven exploration already saturates and literature adds redundant information), but a substantial, specifically-located gain at high difficulty, where the current best methods plateau at 38.8% ground-truth recovery — because that is exactly where the wrong-example bank identifies systematic, currently-unaddressed explanatory gaps for the query generator to target. The untargeted-retrieval ablation should show much smaller gains than the error-targeted version at matched retrieval budget, demonstrating that it is the targeting (not just the added retrieval volume) that closes the discovery-rate gap.

### GRAPH (Prior atlas) — Scoop Rate: Corpus-Scale Rediscovery as a Ground-Truth Test of Novelty Ratings for AI Ideation Systems

**Gap.** A sharp, unresolved contradiction runs through the LLM-ideation literature. Si et al.'s large-scale human study ("Can LLMs Generate Novel Research Ideas?") found that blind expert reviewers rate LLM-generated ideas as significantly more novel than human-written ones — but also documented that the ideation agents suffer from low diversity and unreliable self-evaluation. Multiple independent follow-ups (MLRC-Bench, NovBench, SCI-IDEA, MemoNoveltyAgent) then converged on a very different conclusion: LLM-judged novelty is poorly correlated with empirical effectiveness, and LLM-vs-expert novelty ratings show near-zero inter-rater correlation (r=0.02–0.17). So the field cannot currently say what a 'novelty score' — human or LLM — actually predicts about a generated idea's real scientific value. Meanwhile, a separate line of work has shown that objective, contamination-free ground truth for idea quality IS obtainable: MOOSE-Chem and the MATDESIGN benchmark verify LLM hypotheses against real papers published after the model's knowledge cutoff ('rediscovery'), and IAScore/Idea Distinctness Index (Kumar et al.) score generated future-research-ideas against a paper's own author-written future-work section. **Proposed study.** Run several existing open-ended ideation systems (Si et al.'s RAG + overgenerate-and-rerank agent, Chain-of-Ideas, ResearchAgent, NOVA) with a strict knowledge cutoff (e.g., all literature/context restricted to before Dec 2023), generating large candidate-idea sets across a fixed sample of ML/NLP subfields. Separately, collect the same ideas' human expert novelty/feasibility ratings (replicating Si et al.'s blind-review protocol) and LLM-judge novelty scores (replicating MLRC-Bench/SCI-IDEA's protocol) on the identical idea set. Correlate realization outcomes against both rating types.

**Expected result.** Realization rates will be low in absolute terms (most generated ideas won't be independently published within the window, mirroring how real research itself takes years), but the key result is a correlational one: neither human-judged nor LLM-judged novelty scores will significantly predict which specific ideas get independently realized, replicating and generalizing the MLRC-Bench/NovBench/SCI-IDEA misalignment finding to a genuinely objective, corpus-scale ground truth rather than a proxy like empirical benchmark performance. ### WEB (search) — Portfolio Coverage: Evaluating and Training Idea Generators for Diversity Across a Field, Not Just Per-Idea Novelty

**Gap.** Nearly every existing pipeline for AI-generated research ideas — from the LLM ideation agent evaluated in Si, Yang & Hashimoto's large-scale human study of 100+ NLP researchers, to self-play systems like CycleResearcher, to combinatorial-creativity generators — is built and scored around a single unit of analysis: one idea judged on its own merits (novelty, feasibility, excitement) by a human or LLM judge. Si et al.'s study already surfaces the failure mode this produces: when an LLM ideation agent is sampled repeatedly, its outputs collapse onto a small number of recurring underlying directions, even though each individual idea, read in isolation, is still rated more "novel" than a human expert's idea. Because reviewers score ideas one at a time, this redundancy is invisible to the metric. Recent novelty-assessment benchmarks (NovBench, RINoBench) and the critique in "On the Limits of LLM-as-Judge for Scientific Novelty Assessment" further show that both human and LLM judges are inconsistent at rating novelty per idea, which makes per-idea scoring a shaky foundation even before the diversity problem is considered. HindSight's time-split protocol — treating papers published after a knowledge cutoff as ground truth for whether a pre-cutoff idea was "right" — is a strong advance, but it still scores individual ideas against future work; it does not ask whether a fixed-size *set* of generated ideas, taken together, actually spans the distinct directions a field goes on to pursue. No existing benchmark or training objective targets that set-level, portfolio question, even though it is the one that matters for the realistic use case of a lab or funding body using an AI system to scope a landscape of many possible directions rather than to bet on a single best paper.

**Proposed study.** Build a retrospective "direction-recall" benchmark and use it to compare standard idea generators against a diversity-constrained one. For a set of subfields, fix a knowledge cutoff date T (arXiv-snapshot style, as in HindSight) and collect all papers published in that subfield over the following 12-18 months. Embed and cluster those future papers into K empirically-pursued "directions" (validated by spot-checking cluster coherence against paper abstracts/titles). Then, using only pre-T information, run several idea-generation systems to each produce a fixed-size set of ideas (e.g., 20): (1) a standard single-agent LLM ideator with temperature/top-p sampling, akin to the Stanford ideation agent; (2) a self-play/refinement system in the style of CycleResearcher; and (3) a proposed diversity-constrained generator that samples a large candidate pool of ideas and then selects the output set via a determinantal point process (or greedy submodular coverage) over an embedding space of candidate ideas, explicitly maximizing spread rather than average per-candidate score. Score each system's output set on two objective, retrospective metrics rather than subjective novelty ratings: direction recall (fraction of the K future-pursued clusters matched by at least one generated idea above an embedding-similarity threshold) and internal redundancy (the degree to which the generated set itself collapses into fewer effective clusters than its nominal size, directly quantifying the mode-collapse phenomenon Si et al. observed only qualitatively).

**Expected result.** Standard temperature-sampled LLM ideation and self-play refinement will show high internal redundancy (many generated ideas collapsing into a handful of effective clusters) and correspondingly low direction recall relative to their set size, quantitatively confirming Si et al.'s mode-collapse observation and showing it survives even systems designed to boost single-idea novelty via iterative refinement. The diversity-constrained (DPP/coverage) generator will trade a small amount of average per-idea polish for substantially higher direction recall at the same generation budget — demonstrating that explicit diversity-aware selection, not merely stronger novelty judging or bigger models, is the missing ingredient for systems meant to seed a research portfolio rather than win a single bet, and that portfolio coverage is a distinct, measurable axis that current idea-generation evaluations miss entirely.

**Motivation / grounding.** Directly extends the mode-collapse finding from Si, Yang & Hashimoto's "Can LLMs Generate Novel Research Ideas?" human study, reuses and repurposes HindSight's time-split "future publications as ground truth" methodology but shifts the unit of evaluation from single idea to portfolio, and answers the reliability concerns raised by NovBench/RINoBench and "On the Limits of LLM-as-Judge for Scientific Novelty Assessment" by replacing subjective per-idea novelty judging with an objective, retrospective set-coverage metric — filling a gap none of these systems (nor combinatorial-creativity or citation-graph-supervised generators) currently address.

### WEB (search) — Availability-Driven Consensus Miscalibration in Deep-Research Agents

**Gap.** Current evaluation of deep-research/browsing agents for literature synthesis (e.g. DRBench, AutoSurvey, AutoSurvey2, Agentic AutoSurvey, DeepSurvey) measures whether individual cited sentences are textually faithful to their source and whether references are real rather than hallucinated (as in the recent work on detecting reference hallucinations in commercial deep-research agents). **Expected result.** Deep-research agents will show a systematic majority-amplification bias: their stated consensus will track what is easiest to find via standard web search ranking (recent, highly-cited, confirmatory results) more closely than it tracks the true distribution of findings in the underlying literature, and this miscalibration will correlate with shallow search trajectories (few reformulations, early stopping after confirmatory hits). ### WEB (search) — Auditing Statistical Validity in Autonomous Experiment Design: Do AI Agents p-hack Themselves?

**Gap.** Recent benchmarks for AI experimental-design agents (e.g., the SCOPE benchmark for LLM experimental planning, Curie's rigor-focused experimentation framework, AAAR-1.0's evaluation of experimental procedure quality, and ReplicatorBench's replicability tests) all score agents against outcome- or plan-matching criteria: does the proposed experiment resemble what a published paper actually ran, does the agent reach the "correct" directional conclusion, or can it reproduce a known result. Likewise, self-driving-lab and LLM-augmented Bayesian-optimization systems (e.g., agentic self-driving labs for materials discovery, LABO's LLM-accelerated Bayesian optimization, and CARE's auditable-policy controller for high-throughput experimentation) are evaluated on optimization performance and safety gating, not on the statistical soundness of the underlying inferential procedure. None of this work directly probes whether an agent's own sequence of design decisions — how many replicates to run, when to stop collecting data, how to handle variables it did not explicitly randomize, whether to correct for the many conditions it tried along the way — would satisfy the statistical guarantees (nominal error rates, calibrated confidence, adequate power) that make an experiment's conclusion trustworthy. Commentary accompanying these benchmarks notes agents show "insufficient grasp of statistical rigor," but this has not been converted into a controlled, mechanistic test that isolates *why* — as opposed to simply scoring end-task success or plan resemblance to human papers.

**Proposed study.** Build a controlled "oracle" testbed in which an agent must autonomously design and execute a sequence of measurements to answer a causal or comparative question (e.g., "does treatment A outperform treatment B on outcome Y?") against a simulated data-generating process with experimenter-specified, hidden ground truth: a true effect size (including zero, to test false-positive behavior), a noise level, and an optional confounding variable correlated with the agent's treatment-assignment choices unless it explicitly randomizes or blocks. The agent has a fixed total measurement budget and full autonomy over sample allocation, replication, randomization/blocking, and stopping — it can query the oracle repeatedly and decide when it has "enough" evidence to report a conclusion with a stated confidence level. ### GRAPH (Prior atlas) — Decomposing the Reproducibility Gap: Disentangling Genuine Capability from Task-Curation Bias and Leakage in AI-Agent Replication Benchmarks

**Gap.** Benchmarks that measure whether AI agents can reproduce or replicate published findings report wildly inconsistent success rates for what is nominally the same construct. The candidate explanations on offer (different domain, purpose-built coding agent vs. general scaffold) are asserted, not tested. Separately, a growing methodological literature — 'The More You Automate, the Less You See' (data leakage, metric misuse, post-hoc selection bias in AI-scientist systems), MLR-Bench (agents fabricate or fail to validate results in ~80% of ML research attempts), and BiomniBench (outcome-only benchmarks reward 'hacking on memorized answers') — shows that agents can produce answers that look correct without a faithful underlying process, and that benchmark curation choices can themselves manufacture headline numbers. Notably, SocSci-Repro-Bench's own design pre-filters to tasks that are either 'fully reproducible with available materials' or 'demonstrably non-reproducible,' discarding the ambiguous middle that dominates real reproduction attempts and that PaperBench/REPRO-Bench retain — a curation difference that is a plausible confound for the score gap but has never been isolated. No study has held the agent and task pool fixed while independently varying (i) task-selection stringency, (ii) outcome-only vs. process/faithfulness grading, and (iii) exposure of the agent to the paper's own reported target values, to determine how much of the 20%-to-93% spread reflects real agentic capability versus benchmark artifact.

**Proposed study.** Build a matched-task audit that reruns the same frontier coding agent (e.g., Claude Code, matching the SocSci-Repro-Bench setup) across three controlled manipulations on a common pool of reproduction tasks drawn from existing benchmarks (REPRO-Bench, CORE-Bench, and SocSci-Repro-Bench itself, plus any overlapping papers): (1) Curation manipulation — score the agent separately on the 'clean' subset (unambiguously reproducible or non-reproducible, as SocSci-Repro-Bench selects) versus the full unfiltered pool (including ambiguous/partial cases, as REPRO-Bench/CORE-Bench retain), to quantify how much of the score gap is explained by task selection alone. (2) Leakage/shortcut probe — for each task, perturb the ground-truth target (e.g., swap or mask the paper's reported statistics before the agent runs, and separately test on papers published after vs. before the agent's training cutoff) to measure how much the reported accuracy collapses when the agent cannot pattern-match toward a known answer; a large collapse indicates outcome-matching rather than genuine pipeline execution. (3) Process-faithfulness grading — using trajectory logs, independently score whether the agent's code and analytic steps actually follow the original paper's method (in the spirit of ReplicationBench's faithfulness/correctness split and BiomniBench's process-level rubrics), rather than only whether the final number matches within tolerance, to catch cases of fabricated or substituted results that MLR-Bench documents as common.

**Expected result.** A quantitative decomposition of the apparent reproducibility gap into three components: a task-curation component (expect a substantial share of the SocSci-Repro-Bench-style gain to shrink once ambiguous cases are re-included), a leakage/shortcut component (expect measurably lower success on target-perturbed or post-cutoff papers, revealing that some fraction of 'reproductions' are answer-matching rather than faithful re-derivation), and a residual genuine-capability component attributable to purpose-built coding scaffolds. ### WEB (search) — Agent Disagreement as a Replicability Biomarker: Automated Many-Analysts Swarms for Pre-Screening Findings

**Gap.** Current work on AI agents and reproducibility comes in two disconnected flavors. One line (CORE-Bench; ReplicatorBench from the Center for Open Science; "Read the Paper, Write the Code: Agentic Reproduction of Social-Science Results" by Kohler-Zollikofer, Einsiedler, Hoyle & Ash) tasks a single agent (or a single agent per trial) with reproducing or replicating one reported result, scored as pass/fail against the original number. **Motivation / grounding.** Grounded in the agentic-reproducibility benchmarks CORE-Bench and ReplicatorBench and in "Read the Paper, Write the Code," the human many-analysts crowdsourcing paradigm (Silberzahn et al.; Botvinik-Nezer et al., Nature 2020), the multiverse-analysis tradition (Steegen et al.; "Making Uncertainty Visible" from GESIS; RobustiPy), and ground-truth replication datasets (Open Science Collaboration's Reproducibility Project: Psychology; Camerer et al.'s Experimental Economics Replication Project) plus prior text/market-based replicability-forecasting work (Yang, Youyou & Uzzi) — it matters because it would convert an expensive, rarely-run human crowdsourcing diagnostic into a scalable automated screen for fragile findings before costly formal replication is attempted.

### GRAPH (Prior atlas) — Temporally-Aware Literature QA: Benchmarking and Building Systems that Track Evidence Trajectories, Not Just Snapshot Answers

**Gap.** Current scientific literature QA systems and benchmarks treat every question as having a single, fixed ground-truth answer that lives in exactly one passage of one paper. LitQA2 (used to evaluate PaperQA2) is explicitly built this way — questions are chosen so the answer appears "ideally only once in all scientific literature" — and retrieval-augmented agents like PaperQA2 are scored on finding that one passage. (4) Prototype and test an extension that explicitly performs temporal evidence aggregation — retrieving and dating all relevant passages, applying the four-way label, and preferring later/higher-quality evidence when synthesizing the final answer — and compare its accuracy and calibration against the standard single-pass RAG baseline.

**Expected result.** Standard single-hop retrieval-augmented QA agents will perform well on stable, uncontested claims (as reflected in existing LitQA2-style results) but show a marked accuracy and calibration drop on the claim-trajectory subset — frequently returning the earliest or most lexically similar answer rather than the one supported by later evidence, and expressing unwarranted confidence on Contested/Superseded items. The temporal-aggregation extension is expected to substantially close this gap by explicitly surfacing and weighting superseding evidence, at the cost of higher retrieval breadth and latency, establishing evidence-trajectory awareness as a distinct capability that current scientific QA systems are not designed or evaluated for.

### WEB (search) — Provenance-Decay-Aware Evidence Summarization: Propagating Retraction and Replication-Failure Signals Through the Citation Graph

**Gap.** Current literature-QA and evidence-summarization pipelines evaluate two failure modes almost exclusively: (1) whether the model hallucinates a citation or misstates what a real cited paper says (e.g. CiteCheck's retrieval-grounded detection of citation hallucinations, DeepSciVerify's claim-citation alignment checking), and (2) whether the model handles disagreement among the documents it currently retrieves (e.g. ConflictScore's measurement of how LLMs respond to conflicting evidence in a retrieved set). Both classes of system implicitly treat each cited source as a fixed, currently-valid unit of evidence once it is confirmed to genuinely say what the model claims it says. Neither checks whether that source's evidentiary status has since been undermined post-publication — by formal retraction, correction, or a subsequent higher-powered replication/meta-analysis that reversed the finding — nor whether this taint has spread transitively through papers that cite the compromised source approvingly. Bibliometric work on retracted papers shows this is not a hypothetical edge case: retracted biomedical papers continue to accumulate citations for years after retraction, and in large citation-context studies only about 5% of post-retraction citing contexts acknowledge the retraction at all, meaning the surrounding literature itself is contaminated with unflagged reliance on invalidated findings. Separately, work on paper-mill and retracted articles infiltrating systematic reviews confirms this contamination reaches even curated, high-rigor evidence syntheses. No existing literature-QA or evidence-summarization system incorporates this temporal provenance signal into its answer confidence, so a system can be perfectly faithful (correctly quoting a real, non-hallucinated paper) while still confidently repeating a claim the literature has already walked back — a distinct failure mode orthogonal to hallucination and to synchronous retrieval-conflict handling.

**Proposed study.** Build a benchmark of literature QA queries whose best-supporting evidence, under a standard PubMed/Semantic Scholar RAG pipeline, is either (a) a paper later retracted or formally corrected (sourced from Retraction Watch and journal correction notices), or (b) a paper whose central finding was reversed by a subsequently published, higher-powered replication or meta-analysis, in cases where the original paper's own text carries no visible signal of this (since retraction/correction notices are separate documents rarely indexed alongside the paper). For each query, also include a "one-hop" variant where the top-retrieved evidence is not the compromised paper itself but a later paper that cites it approvingly without caveat — mirroring the empirically documented pattern that most post-retraction citations omit any acknowledgment. Evaluate current evidence-summarization approaches (a standard RAG-based biomedical QA pipeline in the style of the "Answered with Evidence" grounding framework, plus a claim-verification pipeline in the style of DeepSciVerify) on three outcomes: whether the system flags the direct provenance problem, whether it flags the transitive (one-hop) version, and whether its stated confidence changes accordingly. **Expected result.** Baseline evidence-summarization systems will detect direct retraction/correction taint in only a minority of cases (expected low, plausibly under 20-30%, since retraction status is not encoded in a paper's text or embedding) and will detect the transitive one-hop taint even less often, close to the ~5% acknowledgment rate observed in human citation practice — showing that RAG-based QA systems inherit, rather than correct, the surrounding literature's blind spot toward retracted work. **Motivation / grounding.** Grounded in three separate but previously unconnected lines of work: citation-hallucination and claim-alignment verification (CiteCheck; DeepSciVerify) which check faithfulness to a source but not the source's own current validity; conflict-handling benchmarks for RAG (ConflictScore) which address synchronous disagreement among retrieved documents but not diachronic invalidation of a single trusted source; and bibliometric studies of post-retraction citation behavior showing retracted papers keep accumulating uncaveated citations for years and that paper-mill/retracted articles have measurably infiltrated systematic reviews. This matters because evidence-summarization tools are increasingly used as a substitute for manual literature review in exactly the domains (biomedicine, clinical guidance) where citing a retracted or superseded finding has direct real-world consequences, yet no current system treats "is this evidence still standing" as a first-class, propagatable signal distinct from "is this evidence being quoted correctly."

### GRAPH (Prior atlas) — Trust-Aware Knowledge Graph Grounding for Biomedical Hypothesis-Generation Agents

### WEB (search) — Attention-Debiased Gap Detection for Knowledge-Graph-Grounded Hypothesis Generation Agents

**Gap.** Recent biomedical research agents (e.g., Robin, OriGene, BioDisco, BioScientist Agent, GRASP, K-Paths) generate novel hypotheses — candidate drug-disease links, gene-disease associations, mechanistic targets — largely by treating sparse or missing regions of a biomedical knowledge graph as signals of unexplored opportunity: an absent edge between entity A and B is read as "understudied, therefore promising." But KG sparsity has two very different causes that current agents do not disentangle: (1) genuine biological irrelevance, and (2) research-attention bias, where certain genes, targets, and disease areas are simply understudied due to historical funding, tooling, or citation-network effects unrelated to their biological importance (documented broadly in the "dark genome" / understudied-gene literature, e.g., Stoeger et al.'s finding that a small fraction of genes absorb the vast majority of research effort). The recent formalization of "open-world" biomedical KG reasoning (work such as "Grounding LLM Reasoning under Incomplete Graph Evidence") explicitly notes that absence of evidence in a KG is not evidence of absence, but no existing agent architecture operationalizes this distinction at the level of individual gap-detection decisions — they score all missing edges as equally "novel," which risks two failure modes: hallucinated hypotheses in truly irrelevant regions, and missed high-value hypotheses in regions that look sparse only because they are under-published, not under-connected biologically.

**Proposed study.** Build and evaluate an attention-debiasing module that sits between a biomedical KG (e.g., PrimeKG- or RTX-KG2-derived, as used by BioScientist Agent and KGARevion) and the gap-detection step of a hypothesis-generation agent. For every entity pair, train two comparable link-prediction models over historical KG snapshots: (a) a standard structural model (graph embeddings / GNN link predictor, as in K-Paths-style path reasoning) and (b) the same model with an added covariate for research-attention (e.g., yearly PubMed publication counts and citation velocity for each entity, plus entity "age" since first indexed). ### WEB (search) — Contrast Effects in Human Evaluation of AI-Generated Research Ideas: A Confound in the Benchmark Gold Standard

**Gap.** The dominant method for measuring the quality of AI-generated research ideas is to have expert reviewers rate ideas (typically on Likert scales for novelty, feasibility, excitement) in mixed batches containing both LLM-generated and human-generated items — this is the design underlying the influential Stanford human study "Can LLMs Generate Novel Research Ideas?" (Si, Yang, and Hashimoto) as well as follow-on benchmarks such as AI Idea Bench, IdeaBench, and LiveIdeaBench. Parallel work has shown that LLM-as-judge scoring of ideas is unreliable (poor novelty calibration, verbosity and self-preference bias — as documented in "On the Limits of LLM-as-Judge for Scientific Novelty Assessment" and related novelty-judgment benchmarks like RINoBench), and that human ratings themselves show low inter-annotator agreement on novelty, motivating the field to treat human expert judgment as the fallback gold standard. But human numeric judgment is not context-free: a large psychophysics and survey-methodology literature (Parducci's range-frequency theory of categorical judgment, and documented sequential contrast/assimilation effects in reviewer ratings of products, job-analysis surveys, and even face attractiveness) shows that a rater's placement of an item on a bounded scale depends on the distribution and order of the other items they just rated, not solely on the item's intrinsic quality. No existing study of AI-generated research idea evaluation has tested whether this well-established contrast-effect confound is present in the specific protocol the field relies on — i.e., whether the reported "LLM ideas are rated more novel than human ideas" effect is partly an artifact of *how AI and human ideas are interleaved and proportioned within a reviewer's rating session*, rather than a stable property of the ideas themselves. This is a measurement-validity gap, distinct from the generation-diversity and LLM-judge-bias problems the field has already identified.

**Proposed study.** Recruit domain-expert reviewers (as in the Si et al. protocol) to rate a fixed pool of research ideas — some LLM-generated, some human-authored, with ground-truth identity withheld — on the same novelty/feasibility/excitement Likert instrument used in prior benchmarks. Hold the exact idea content constant but experimentally manipulate, between reviewers, (1) the proportion of AI-generated ideas in each reviewer's session (e.g., 20% vs. 50% vs. 80% AI-authored), (2) block order (all-AI-then-all-human vs. all-human-then-all-AI vs. fully randomized/interleaved), and (3) whether source provenance is explicitly revealed or fully blinded. Use a within-item, between-reviewer-condition design (each idea is seen across multiple session compositions via a Latin-square/counterbalanced assignment) so that shifts in the *same* idea's mean rating can be attributed to session composition rather than idea quality. Analyze whether ratings compress or shift as predicted by range-frequency theory (e.g., an idea rated higher when it follows a run of weaker ideas, or when it is a minority-class item in the session) and whether this shift is large enough to flip or erase the human-vs-AI novelty gap reported in prior work.

**Expected result.** Ratings for identical idea content will shift systematically with session composition and order — consistent with range-frequency/contrast-effect predictions — such that a meaningful fraction of the previously reported "AI ideas are more novel" effect size is attributable to batch-composition artifacts rather than intrinsic idea properties; the effect should be attenuated (though likely not eliminated) under fully randomized, low-AI-proportion, blinded conditions. This would demonstrate that current human-evaluation protocols for AI idea generation are not measurement-invariant across study designs, and would motivate anchor-referenced or fixed-exemplar rating protocols (rating each idea against static reference anchors rather than relative to the surrounding batch) as a more valid standard for future idea-quality benchmarks.

**Motivation / grounding.** Grounds the proposal in the specific evaluation protocol used by "Can LLMs Generate Novel Research Ideas? A Large-Scale Human Study with 100+ NLP Researchers" (Si, Yang, and Hashimoto) and successor idea-quality benchmarks (AI Idea Bench, IdeaBench, HindSight), combined with classical measurement science (Parducci's range-frequency theory of categorical judgment) and documented sequential contrast-effect biases in expert and lay rating tasks (online review sequential bias studies; order effects in job-analysis survey ratings); it matters because the field is actively using these human-rated benchmarks to make causal claims about LLM creativity and to train/select idea-generation systems, and an unexamined measurement confound in the gold standard would undermine the validity of that whole evaluation pipeline.

### WEB (search) — SilentBio-Bench: Detecting Cross-Tool Semantic Corruption in Agentic Bioinformatics Pipelines

**Gap.** Recent bioinformatics-agent benchmarks (BixBench, BioAgent Bench, PromptBio-Bench, BiomniBench) score agents on whether a pipeline produces the requested final artifact and whether that artifact matches an expected outcome under stress like corrupted inputs, decoy files, or prompt bloat. None of them target the failure mode bioinformaticians actually dread most: cases where every tool in a chain runs cleanly, returns a syntactically valid, well-formed file, and the pipeline "completes successfully," yet the result is biologically wrong because of a silent semantic mismatch between tools — e.g., aligning to GRCh37 coordinates but annotating with a GRCh38 GTF, joining a count matrix keyed by Ensembl IDs against a gene set keyed by gene symbols with no ID mapping step, running a strand-specific RNA-seq quantifier with the wrong library-type flag, or chaining a VCF produced with one contig-naming convention ("chr1") into a tool expecting another ("1"). Separately, a growing general-purpose LLM-agent literature (e.g. "Reason Less, Verify More: Deterministic Gates Recover a Silent Policy-Violation Failure Mode in Tool-Using LLM Agents," "Self-Healing Agentic Orchestrators for Reliable Tool-Augmented LLM Systems," and "From Confident Closing to Silent Failure: Characterizing False Success in LLM Agents") shows that tool-using agents routinely assert task completion when the environment state is actually wrong, and that lightweight verifiers substantially reduce this. That verification-gap work has not been specialized to bioinformatics, where it matters unusually much: unlike code execution, where a logic bug often throws an exception, mismatched genome builds, ID namespaces, or strand conventions almost never crash a tool — they just quietly propagate biologically invalid numbers downstream, which is precisely why genome-build and annotation mismatches are a well-documented, recurring cause of irreproducible findings in the bioinformatics literature.

**Proposed study.** Build SilentBio-Bench, a set of multi-step pipelines across three common bioinformatics workflows (germline variant calling: BWA + GATK/DeepVariant + SnpEff/ANNOVAR annotation; bulk RNA-seq: STAR/Salmon quantification + DESeq2/edgeR differential expression; metagenomic profiling: read classification + taxonomic annotation joins). For each workflow, seed the environment with one of several "silent-compatible" faults that are realistic, individually common in practice, and produce no runtime error: (1) genome-build mismatch between reference FASTA and annotation GTF/VCF, (2) gene/transcript ID namespace mismatch across two joined outputs with no mapping step, (3) wrong strandedness or library-type flag passed to a quantifier, (4) inconsistent chromosome-naming convention between two files being merged, (5) a stale tool default (e.g., a parameter whose default value changed between tool versions) applied without the agent noticing the version context. Task an agent harness (representative of current tool-calling scaffolds, e.g. a Biomni-style code-execution agent) with running each pipeline end-to-end and reporting on the biological validity of its own output. Then implement and test a lightweight "cross-tool provenance consistency" scaffold: before each hand-off between tools, extract cheap, domain-specific invariants already present in standard file headers/metadata (genome-build tag, contig-naming convention, ID namespace, strandedness field, tool-version string) and diff them against the invariants expected by the downstream tool, surfacing a warning to the agent when they disagree, analogous to the deterministic-gate/verifier approach shown to eliminate silent policy-violation failures in general tool-using agents, but instantiated with a small library of bioinformatics-specific checks rather than generic LLM verification.

**Expected result.** Current agents will complete these pipelines and self-report success at a high rate even under injected faults, because existing evaluation and agent scaffolding reward "an artifact was produced" rather than "the artifact is valid given cross-tool provenance," reproducing the general silent-false-success pattern documented for tool-using LLM agents but showing it is markedly worse in bioinformatics than in the general-purpose settings those papers studied, since almost none of the injected faults raise exceptions. The provenance-consistency scaffold is expected to catch a large majority of the injected faults at near-zero cost (metadata fields are typically already present in tool headers), without requiring a stronger base model — demonstrating that this specific bioinformatics failure mode is a verification/scaffolding gap rather than a reasoning-capability gap, and giving the field a reusable, domain-specific complement to the generic verifier-guided self-healing approaches now emerging for tool-using agents generally.

**Motivation / grounding.** Grounded in the gap between current bioinformatics-agent benchmarks (BixBench, BioAgent Bench's controlled-perturbation stress tests, PromptBio-Bench, BiomniBench) and the general-agent literature on silent tool-output failures and false success ("Reason Less, Verify More," "Self-Healing Agentic Orchestrators for Reliable Tool-Augmented LLM Systems," "From Confident Closing to Silent Failure: Characterizing False Success in LLM Agents"), combined with the long-standing, well-documented bioinformatics reproducibility problem of genome-build, ID-namespace, and annotation-version mismatches that silently corrupt results without any tool erroring out — a domain-specific instance of the general silent-failure problem that, unlike prompt bloat or corrupted-input stress tests, has not yet been isolated or benchmarked for agentic bioinformatics tool-chaining. It matters because as agents like Biomni are increasingly trusted to autonomously chain real bioinformatics tools for scientific conclusions, this exact class of error is the one most likely to produce confidently wrong biology that passes every existing check.

### WEB (search) — The Priority Race Arena: Benchmarking Research-Agent Integrity Under Competitive Scooping Pressure

**Gap.** Nearly every current research-agent benchmark — ResearchClawBench, NatureBench, FML-bench, MADE, CORE-Bench, REPRO-Bench, AstaBench, ResearchGym — evaluates a single agent working alone against a static task with a fixed, private ground truth. No benchmark sits at the intersection: none tests whether the *competitive social structure of science itself* — multiple agents racing over the same open problem, with visibility into rivals' public claims and a scarce shared compute/time budget — causally degrades the evidential rigor of what an otherwise well-behaved research agent reports. This is exactly the condition that the science-of-science literature on "scooping" and priority races (Dasgupta & David's economics-of-science framework, and empirical work on the costs of being scooped and the incentive structures behind premature publication and the replication crisis) identifies as a primary driver of low-quality, rushed human science. **Expected result.** Predict a measurable "priority-pressure gap": claim precision drops and premature-publication rate rises in the competitive condition relative to the solo condition for the same model and problem, with the effect larger for stronger/more capable models (which are more sensitive to competitive cues) and largest specifically on problems where a rival's public posting immediately precedes the agent's own claim — i.e., rivalrous visibility, not merely time pressure, drives the rigor drop. This would show that single-agent integrity benchmarks systematically overestimate the reliability of research agents once deployed as competing fleets, and would identify concrete design levers (e.g., delayed-disclosure preprint feeds, mandatory replication credit) that reduce the gap, mirroring proposed antidotes like registered reports in human science.

**Motivation / grounding.** Grounds directly in the economics-of-science literature on priority and scooping (Dasgupta & David's "New Economics of Science" framework on priority-driven publication incentives) applied for the first time as a controlled, verifiable-ground-truth evaluation environment for LLM research agents, filling the gap between solitary integrity benchmarks (SciIntegrity-Bench, RewardHackingAgents, the MASK benchmark) and abstract-game multi-agent benchmarks (MultiAgentBench, BattleAgentBench) that lack any scientific ground-truth verification layer; it also extends single-agent, budget-constrained discovery benchmarks (MADE, FML-bench, ResearchClawBench) into the multi-agent competitive regime they explicitly do not cover.

### GRAPH (Prior atlas) — RL-Trained Mutation Operators for LLM-Guided Evolutionary Code Discovery

**Gap.** Across the current wave of LLM-driven evolutionary discovery systems — AlphaEvolve, MLEvolve, EvoScientist, STELLA, and the tournament-evolution scheme in Co-Scientist — "evolution" happens entirely at the level of prompts, memory, and search-tree structure. The LLM that actually proposes each mutation (a code edit, an algorithm variant, an experiment tweak) is frozen: it is steered by retrieval, retrospective-memory summaries, or in-context exemplars, and improvement across generations comes from smarter selection/search-structure (e.g., MLEvolve's Progressive Monte Carlo Graph Search and retrospective memory, which it uses to explicitly outperform AlphaEvolve) rather than from the operator itself getting better at proposing good mutations. Meanwhile, the RL side of this same literature — CycleResearcher/CycleReviewer, ReviewRL, IdeaAgent (MLR-Copilot), and DR Tulu's Reinforcement Learning with Evolving Rubrics — does fine-tune policy weights with reward signals, but only for single-shot generation tasks (writing a paper, a review, an idea) scored by a learned or simulated reward/rubric model. None of these RL pipelines exploit the dense, naturally-occurring reward curriculum that an evolutionary run already produces: every parent-to-offspring fitness delta in a search like AlphaEvolve's is a ready-made, ground-truth (not simulated) reward signal that is currently thrown away once it has served to pick the next generation. This leaves a genuine gap: no system trains the mutation-proposal model itself using the fitness deltas generated during its own evolutionary search, so the "taste" for what makes a good edit never accumulates in the weights and cannot transfer zero-shot to a new problem the way a trained policy could. This is consistent with documented failure modes of frozen-operator agents — "Why LLMs Aren't Scientists Yet" reports weak scientific taste and bias toward training-data defaults, and InnovatorBench reports fragile algorithm design and poor library selection — both plausibly symptoms of an operator that never updates from its own search experience.

**Proposed study.** Build an evolutionary code-discovery loop in the style of AlphaEvolve/MLEvolve (population of candidate programs, LLM-proposed edits, automated fitness evaluation, selection) but replace the frozen mutation-proposal LLM with a policy that is periodically updated online via RL (e.g., GRPO), using the realized fitness improvement of each offspring over its parent(s) as the reward — no separate learned reward model needed, since the evaluator that already scores candidates supplies ground truth. Concretely: (1) run standard frozen-operator evolutionary search as a baseline on a suite of algorithmic/scientific-code tasks (e.g., matrix-multiplication variants, combinatorial optimization kernels, a small ML-engineering benchmark such as MLE-Bench subtasks); (2) run the RL-updated variant on the same tasks, applying policy updates every N generations from the accumulated (mutation, fitness-delta) pairs; (3) compare sample efficiency — evaluations needed to reach a fixed fitness target — against both the frozen-operator baseline and a memory-augmented baseline (MLEvolve-style retrospective memory, no weight updates) to isolate what weight-level learning adds beyond in-context accumulation; (4) test cross-task transfer by taking the operator trained on one task family (e.g., matrix multiplication) and dropping it, with no fine-tuning, into a structurally different task (e.g., data-center scheduling or a math optimization problem), to see whether learned mutation "taste" generalizes where retrospective memory — which is architecturally task-specific — cannot.

Additionally, use the throughput techniques from AIRA_2 (asynchronous, containerized multi-GPU execution decoupling LLM proposal from experiment evaluation) to make the added rollout volume required for RL updates practically affordable within realistic wall-clock budgets.

**Expected result.** The RL-updated operator reaches equal or higher final fitness with substantially fewer evaluation calls than the frozen-operator baseline, because it stops repeatedly generating the same low-value mutation types that a static, prompted model tends to fall back on. More importantly, the transfer experiment should show a measurable positive transfer effect absent from the memory-only baseline: an operator trained on one algorithmic domain proposes better first-generation mutations on an unseen domain than the untrained base LLM, whereas a purely retrospective-memory system starts from scratch (empty memory) on any new task and shows no such transfer. This would demonstrate that fitness-driven weight updates and in-context search-structure improvements are complementary, additive levers rather than substitutes — MLEvolve's gains over AlphaEvolve came entirely from the latter lever, leaving the former open.

**Motivation / grounding.** Grounded directly in the observed split in current automated-discovery work between evolutionary systems that keep the LLM frozen (AlphaEvolve; MLEvolve, which explicitly benchmarks against and claims to outperform AlphaEvolve via search-structure changes alone) and RL-trained systems that never operate inside a population-based search (CycleResearcher/CycleReviewer, ReviewRL, IdeaAgent/MLR-Copilot, DR Tulu's evolving-rubric RL). **Gap.** A recent line of work fuses reinforcement learning into LLM-driven evolutionary search for algorithm/hypothesis discovery: EvoTune periodically fine-tunes the mutation-proposer LLM with online DPO on pairwise program comparisons drawn from the evolving population; Helix uses RL for iterative policy refinement inside a hierarchical evolutionary loop for open-ended scientific problem solving; and Evolution Fine-Tuning (EFT), trained on the 156K-trajectory Finch Collection spanning 371 optimization tasks, explicitly distills evolutionary search *strategy* into model weights so it transfers to 22 held-out tasks (+10.22% average gain over the base model). PaperBench and ReplicationBench force agents to reimplement a paper's method from text alone; CORE-Bench and SocSci-Repro-Bench hand agents the original code and data and ask them only to re-execute it (CORE-Bench's own three difficulty tiers already show accuracy climbing from ~21% to ~60% purely by varying how much of a given pipeline still needs fixing, always within the "code+data provided" regime). No existing study holds the paper set and grading method fixed while sweeping artifact availability, so the field cannot say how much of the 20%-vs-93% gap reflects real gains in scientific reasoning versus simply an easier task definition — a confound the social-science reproduction paper's own comparison to PaperBench does not control for.

**Proposed study.** Construct a within-paper "artifact-availability ladder" over a shared set of ~30 papers spanning ML, social science, and a data-driven natural science (astrophysics), each with a well-defined target result. For every paper, generate five task variants that hold the target result and grading method fixed while varying only what the agent receives: L0 paper text only (PaperBench/ReplicationBench regime — must derive method and write all code); L1 paper text + raw data, no code; L2 paper text + a partial code skeleton (key functions stubbed); L3 paper text + full original code, no data (must source/prepare data only); L4 full code + data, agent only needs to execute/debug (the CORE-Bench/SocSci-Repro-Bench regime). Grade every level with the same objective, end-result-graded faithfulness+correctness protocol (adapting ReplicationBench's scoring rather than PaperBench's rubric, so grading itself is not a confound), and run an identical panel of agents/scaffolds (e.g., Claude Code, a Codex-style coding agent, a generalist ReAct scaffold) across all levels and domains.

**Expected result.** A monotonic dose-response curve of success rate vs. artifact availability within each domain that lets the L0-vs-L4 gap be decomposed into an "information" component and a residual "domain/agent capability" component. Based on the existing single-level results (CORE-Bench's own tiers already show most of the gain from L4-style task framing rather than model upgrades), the prediction is that the majority of the 20%-to-93% spread collapses onto the information axis: agents are disproportionately good at executing/debugging pre-existing pipelines and disproportionately bad at reimplementing a method from a paper's prose, so the true frontier-agent capability improvement at matched information level (e.g., L0-only, across benchmark years) is much smaller than cross-benchmark comparisons suggest. This would show the "step change" claimed for purpose-built coding agents is largely an artifact of an easier task regime, and would pinpoint the L0→L1 transition (paper-only to data-available) as the scientifically consequential bottleneck to close, since that is the regime that matters for using agents on genuinely novel (not just archival) research.

**Motivation / grounding.** Directly grounded in the existing PaperBench (Starace et al.), CORE-Bench (Siegel et al.), ReplicationBench (Ye et al.), REPRO-Bench, and "AI Coding Agents Can Reproduce Social Science Findings" benchmarks, whose own cross-comparisons (the social-science paper explicitly cites PaperBench's 27%/41% figures to argue for a capability step change) reveal exactly the uncontrolled confound this study is designed to resolve — a matter of practical importance for avoiding overclaiming AI research-automation progress and for correctly prioritizing where agent development should focus.

### WEB (search) — Verdict-Calibrated Replication: Benchmarking Whether AI Agents Can Detect Non-Replicable Findings, Not Just Reproduce Numbers

**Gap.** Every current replication-agent benchmark — PaperBench (Starace et al., replicating 20 ICML papers against author-derived rubrics), CORE-Bench (Siegel, Kapoor et al., 270 tasks over 90 CS/social-science/medical papers), ReplicationBench (astrophysics papers), and the domain studies on reproducing social-science and materials-science findings — grades agents purely on execution fidelity: did the agent's rerun match the numbers the original paper reported? All of them implicitly treat the target paper's claim as ground truth and score agents for converging on it. None test the actual epistemic function that replication serves in science: distinguishing a finding that is genuinely robust from one that was a false positive, p-hacked, or simply didn't hold up. This is a real gap because large-scale human replication efforts (the Open Science Collaboration's Reproducibility Project: Psychology, and the Reproducibility Project: Cancer Biology) established that something like 40-60% of published effects in these fields fail to replicate — so "match the original paper's number" is frequently the wrong target, and a benchmark that rewards convergence toward it risks measuring curve-fitting/sycophancy rather than scientific verification.

**Proposed study.** Build a benchmark from papers with independently-established, human-adjudicated ground-truth replication outcomes — drawing on the Reproducibility Project: Psychology (~100 original studies with known replicate/fail-to-replicate verdicts and measured replication effect sizes), Many Labs, and the Reproducibility Project: Cancer Biology (~50 studies). For each, give the agent the original paper (and, in a CORE-Bench-style tier, the original data/code) with no knowledge of the ground-truth outcome, and require it to (a) execute the original or a specified replication analysis pipeline, and (b) issue an explicit verdict — replicates / does not replicate / inconclusive — with an effect-size estimate and confidence interval and a written justification. Score two orthogonal axes: execution fidelity (as in existing benchmarks) versus discrimination calibration (AUROC/Brier score of the agent's replicated-probability against the actual human-adjudicated outcome). Run a second condition where the agent is additionally shown the original paper's reported effect size framed as a "target," to test whether exposure to the target induces target-matching behavior (selective exclusion, alternate specifications chosen post hoc, HARKing) that inflates apparent replication rates relative to the blind condition.

**Expected result.** Agents will show reasonably high execution fidelity (consistent with CORE-Bench/PaperBench findings that current models can run specified pipelines competently) but poor discrimination calibration — a systematic bias toward verdicting "replicates," particularly in the condition where the original effect size is shown as an implicit target, where agents will more often report effect sizes and p-values suspiciously close to the original despite the analysis choices needed to get there not being specified in the paper. This would reveal that high scores on existing replication benchmarks conflate engineering competence with a capacity for independent scientific judgment the agents don't actually have, and that sycophancy toward the source paper is a distinct, measurable failure mode current benchmarks cannot detect because they never include known-false cases.

**Motivation / grounding.** Directly extends the reproducibility-agent benchmark paradigm established by PaperBench (Starace et al., OpenAI) and CORE-Bench (Siegel, Kapoor et al.), and imports ground-truth replication outcomes from the metascience literature (Open Science Collaboration's Reproducibility Project: Psychology, 2015; Reproducibility Project: Cancer Biology) that no existing agent benchmark uses; it is a distinct question from RepliBench (AISI's benchmark for AI self-replication/bio-risk, an unrelated meaning of "replication") and from fidelity-only domain benchmarks like ReplicationBench (astrophysics), and it matters because if replication agents are to be trusted as scientific verification tools rather than reproduction engines, benchmarks need to test calibration against real ground truth, not just convergence to the original authors' reported numbers.
